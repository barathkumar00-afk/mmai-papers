# COPD Exacerbation — Local Reference Note

PROVENANCE: Local reference note authored for the MedExplain AI research prototype.
Not a published guideline, not a verbatim society document, no PMID or DOI.
Included so the retrieval pipeline has real local material to search.

## Definition

A COPD exacerbation is an **acute worsening of respiratory symptoms** — increased
dyspnoea, increased sputum volume, or increased sputum purulence — that is
acute in onset and requires a change in regular medication. The cardinal point
is the *change from baseline*: a patient breathing normally at their usual
sputum volume with a chronic baseline dyspnoea is not, by definition, in
exacerbation.

## Anthracosis and chronic parenchymal change

Anthracosis is the accumulation of carbon pigment in lung tissue, usually
peribronchial. On plain radiography it manifests as:

- fine nodular or reticulonodular opacities, predominantly **upper lobe**
- more prominent with advancing age
- co-existing hyperinflation: flattened hemidiaphragms, increased retrosternal
  airspace, horizontally oriented ribs, reduced peripheral vascular markings

Anthracosis is a *nonspecific marker of long-term particulate exposure*,
frequently co-dated. It is associated with but not specific to COPD, and it does
not by itself establish COPD as the cause of an acute presentation.

## Hyperinflation

The radiographic features of hyperinflation are listed above. Important
caveat: hyperinflation is **also** seen in asthma and in obesity, and it can be
over-estimated by poor inspiratory effort and by AP projection.

## Gas exchange

Exacerbations frequently cause hypoxaemia with or without a raised PaCO2.
A normal PaCO2 in a patient with a known history of hypercapnic failure is
alarming rather than reassuring, because it may represent over-ventilation from
hypoxaemia. PaCO2 must be interpreted against the patient's own baseline.

## Inflammatory markers

C-reactive protein often rises during exacerbations, particularly those with
purulent sputum. Elevated CRP has been associated with a longer time to
recovery in some cohorts, but it is not sufficiently specific to guide
antibiotic use on its own; sputum purulence and clinical assessment remain the
primary drivers of the antibiotic decision.

## Antibiotic stewardship

Antibiotics are most clearly indicated when all three cardinal features are
present (increased dyspnoea, increased sputum volume, increased sputum purulence)
or when two are present with one being purulence, or when mechanical
ventilation is required. Inpatient cultures — particularly blood cultures —
are obtained before treatment in severe exacerbations. De-escalation should be
planned from the outset.
